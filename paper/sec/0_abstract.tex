\begin{abstract}
Heavy-tailed self-regularization (HTSR) reads the quality of a trained layer from the power-law exponent $\alpha$ of its weight spectrum, and has been used to set layer-wise learning rates and pruning ratios. We ask whether $\alpha$ can also \emph{certify}, \emph{select} and \emph{tune} small spiking inserts in an existing dense model, using a pretrained CLIP ViT-B/32 as the test bed. We (i) profile $\alpha$ for all 148 matrices of the model; (ii) convert the MLP activation of one block at a time into a rate-coded integrate-and-fire population and relate the resulting zero-shot and retrieval damage to the block's $\alpha$; (iii) fine-tune the converted or added spiking block under a distillation objective and track $\alpha$, firing statistics and downstream metrics jointly; (iv) convert blocks cumulatively in $\alpha$-ordered, depth-ordered and random orders; and (v) compare spiking lookup-memory and MLP adapters on frozen embeddings. Two methodological findings are needed to make any of this work on CLIP: a per-channel threshold (the blocks have massive-activation channels that a pooled threshold clips) and a per-channel bias correction (only 2--7\,\% of MLP pre-activations are positive, so a ReLU-like rate code without the GELU negative lobe destroys 20--25 points of zero-shot accuracy, which the correction reduces to 1--2 points at $T{=}4$). Results: $\alpha$ certifies fresh matrices (a learning adapter converges to $\alpha\approx2$ and its $\alpha$ tracks the gain at Spearman $-0.73$; an unneeded insert drifts to 5--6) but not the repair of a pretrained block, whose $\alpha$ moves by $<0.05$ while accuracy recovers up to 9.5 points, independently of surrogate slope, time budget or learning rate. As a selection signal its sign is the opposite of the hypothesis: blocks with $\alpha$ closest to 2 are the most damaged by conversion (Spearman $-0.75$), the ``closest-to-2 first'' order is the worst of six at every fraction (15.9 vs 7.9 points for its reverse at 9 blocks), and damage compounds super-additively in early blocks. A spiking lookup memory beats a dense one but not a spiking MLP adapter. Code, caches and all run logs are released.
\end{abstract}
